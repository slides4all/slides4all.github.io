Common mistakes

These are the problems that come up most often in reviews, with the wrong and the fixed version side by side. The examples were recreated from scratch: no deck from the gallery is used here as a negative case.

Small type in the body

By far the most common problem. In a large room, the people at the back simply do not read it. The phone test at 50 cm catches this in seconds.

Go up to 24pt or more. If it does not fit, cut items, never the type size.

Gray or colored background

A colored background cuts contrast and tires the eyes. When the background is close to the text color the damage is severe, and projectors vary far more than your screen does.

White background, black text. Save color for highlighting one number.

A block of text beside the chart

The audience tries to read the text and look at the chart at the same time, and does neither. The chart gets squeezed on top of that.

One large chart alone on the slide. The text becomes your voice, or a slide of its own.

A small figure with space to spare

Leftover white space almost always means the figure could be larger. Slide space is precious and it is being wasted.

Scale the figure to the edge of the slide. If it is complex, let it take 100%, over the title and the footer.

A complex figure on a single slide

While you explain step 1, half the room is already trying to decode step 4. You lose their attention exactly where it matters most.

Break it across slides, one step at a time, adding content with each slide.

Decorative numbers in boxes

The big number takes the best space in the box and says nothing. Anyone reading can already see there are three contributions.

Drop the number and use the space for the keyword of each contribution, in larger type.

Text touching or overflowing the edge

It looks visibly careless and, on a projector, the last line sometimes disappears entirely. It is the kind of detail an audience notices before the content.

Give the text room inside the box and check every edge, one by one.

Two languages mixed together

Switching language mid-phrase stalls the reader. It is usually accidental, which reads as carelessness.

Pick one language and hold it from the first slide to the last, including chart and table labels.

Inconsistent abbreviation

If one term is abbreviated and another at the same level is not, the audience looks for meaning in the difference. There is none, and attention is lost on the way.

Either abbreviate everything or nothing. The same goes for color, alignment and size of equivalent elements.

A table copied from the paper

The table in the paper was made to be read at 30 cm, on paper, at your own pace. On a slide it becomes a gray smudge of unreadable numbers.

Rebuild it for the slide: fewer columns, fewer rows, large type, only what supports the argument.

Related work that only lists papers

A list of papers tells the audience nothing. What they need to see is the gap your work fills.

Choose the columns from your own focus and make the gap visible. If the table comes early, call it what exists and what is missing.

Tiny logos on the first slide

The first slide stays up while you are being introduced, which is exactly when the institutions could be recognized. With tiny logos, nobody recognizes anything.

Make the logos on the opening slide considerably larger.

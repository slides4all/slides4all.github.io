How to present

Presenting your work means making the audience understand your argument. They do not have to agree. They do have to understand.

Rehearse, at least five times

Rehearsing is not reading the slides silently. It is standing up, speaking out loud and running a clock.

  • Experience shows that only from the fifth or sixth run does the delivery become fluent, well organized and free of repetition.
  • Rehearsing fixes the content in memory, calms you down and builds a logical, natural sequence.
  • If you can, record audio or video of your rehearsals and listen back later. You will notice things you missed while recording.

The audience can tell when there was no rehearsal

It is not about talent. The signs are always the same.

  • The speaker stumbles and keeps pausing to work out what to say next.
  • They repeat what they already said, having found a better way to explain something that should already be clear.
  • They fail to deliver the most important message within the time.
  • They get cut off before finishing, for running over.
  • They run out of time for questions and discussion, which are among the greatest benefits of presenting.

During the talk

  • Look at the audience. The talk is not for the screen, the laptop, the floor or the ceiling.
  • If you are shy, move your gaze across the room, or fix a point at the back, slightly above people's heads.
  • Do not read the slides. It signals lack of preparation immediately, and if you rehearsed you will not need to: seeing the slide brings the content back.
  • Equally, do not talk about a pile of things that are not on the slide. People get lost when they cannot find visual support for what they are hearing.
  • The balance: the slide carries the essentials, your voice adds the fuller sentences.
  • If you spend two minutes or more on one slide, it probably has too much on it and should become two or three.
  • It is tiring to sit through a talk where the slide almost never changes.

The time belongs to the event, not to you

Respect, respect and respect the time you were allotted.

  • In rehearsal, aim to land within 5% of your allotted time, over or under, not counting questions.
  • Never go past 10% on the day. You can lose the right to finish.
  • Running over forces the session chair to cut you off, and shows disregard for the rules, the organizers and the audience.
  • Finishing far too early is also bad: it wastes valuable time you were given and suggests you had nothing to explain.
  • If you are unsure how long your slot is, ask the chair of your track or of the event.

Suggested split of the time

The audience came for the problem and for your solution. Long background eats exactly the time those two parts need.

  • 10% for background, only enough to situate the work
  • 25% for the problem and the challenges, with the technical detail
  • 55% for the solution and the results, with limitations and discussion
  • 10% for closing remarks, future work and acknowledgements

Before you call it done

  • Review the content with your co-authors at least twice.
  • Iterate on the slides several times. Like good writing, a good talk takes cycles of refinement.
  • Ask someone who did not work on the deck to review it, on a phone.
  • Run the whole checklist, slide by slide.

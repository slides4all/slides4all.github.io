How to build the slides

Slides support what you say. They are not the paper projected on a wall. Everything here serves one goal: the person at the back of the room has to be able to read and understand.

1. Type size and legibility

By far the most common problem in every review. If you only fix one thing, fix this one.

  • Use 24pt or larger for slide content, including text inside images and tables.
  • Use 32pt or larger for slide titles.
  • Run the phone test: open the deck on your phone, about 50 cm from your eyes. If you had to strain, the type is too small.
  • Always assume a large, crowded room. The people at the back need to follow too.
  • When the content does not fit at a large size, cut the content. Never shrink the type to make it fit.
  • Prefer fewer items, shorter phrases and more slides over one dense slide in small type.
  • Do not leave large empty areas: it usually means the type or the figure could be bigger.

2. Figures and charts

The top priority is that the figure can be seen, not that the template's framing survives.

  • Figures and charts should take up as much of the slide as possible.
  • Complex figures can and should use the entire slide, including 100% of it, covering the title and the footer.
  • Present complex figures progressively, across several slides, one step at a time.
  • If a figure shows an architecture, a set of steps or a flow and you will discuss each part, give each part its own slide.
  • Every experiment has a pipeline or a flowchart. Show that figure: it explains the approach better than any list.
  • Prefer vector or high-resolution images, such as SVG, PDF or a large PNG.
  • Screenshots of regions of a screen are the worst option. If zooming in shows blur or blocky artifacts, replace the image.
  • Remove decorative figures. If you have to choose between keeping a secondary figure and enlarging the type, enlarge the type.
  • Use rectangles, circles and arrows to highlight the part you are explaining at that moment.

3. Text

A slide is not prose. Every extra line competes for attention with what you are saying.

  • Prefer short, direct items that act as a guide for your delivery.
  • Do not transcribe sentences or paragraphs from the paper onto the slide.
  • Avoid complete sentences with subject, verb, object and complements. That is for papers, not for slides.
  • Never put a block of text next to a chart. It clutters the slide and gets in the way of reading the figure.
  • Avoid strong claims without evidence, such as proven, guaranteed or irrefutable. Is there a formal mathematical proof? If not, change the word.
  • Keep one language throughout. Do not mix, as in writing feature with HIGH variância.
  • Remove decorative numbers from boxes and use that space to highlight the keywords.

The headline technique

The simplest way to carry content across is to write like a newspaper headline, and leave the full explanation to your voice.

Sentence from a paper

The new XPTO system developed in this work achieved performance that exceeded the best version in the literature by 30%.

Slide headline

XPTO: 30% faster than the state of the art

4. Visuals

The rule is always the same: anything that does not help understanding gets in the way.

  • White background, black text. Always avoid gray or colored backgrounds: white helps legibility.
  • Do not put colored backgrounds behind text, not even in boxes or panels. If the background color is close to the text color, the damage is worse.
  • Do not overdo colors, except when you need to tell data series apart in a chart.
  • Use color schemes with good contrast.
  • Check the edges of every box and callout: text must not touch or overflow the margin.
  • Enlarge the logos on the first slide. They are usually far too small to be recognized.
  • Number your slides. It helps the audience take notes and aim their questions, and it helps you track your time.
  • A title on every slide is recommended but not mandatory: sometimes enlarging the image is worth more than adding a title.

5. Consistency

Visual consistency has to hold from the first slide to the last. These questions catch nearly every slip.

  • Why is one term abbreviated while another of the same kind is not?
  • Why is one label in one language and another in a different one?
  • Why is one font larger than another for elements at the same level?
  • Why do the same elements use different colors on different slides?
  • Why is there a difference in alignment, spacing, size or style between equivalent elements?

6. Tables and related work

A table copied from the paper almost never works on a slide. It has to be rebuilt.

  • Rebuild the table for the slide instead of copying the one from the paper.
  • Use large type in the table body, not only in the header.
  • Check the header: an awkward line break in a column title is the most common defect.
  • You rarely need the whole table. Show only the most relevant data.
  • You rarely need every table from the paper. Explaining the important numbers well beats rushing through all of them.
  • Reveal the table progressively, highlighting the row or column you are explaining.

The related work table

It is not there to list papers. It is there to make the gap your work fills obvious.

  • Make clear what is missing, or what the limitations of previous work are.
  • Choose the columns from your own focus: if you evaluate balancing techniques, the table has to show which techniques each work used and on what kind of data.
  • If the table appears early in the talk, right after the problem and the challenges, a better title is what exists and what is missing.
  • Do not spend much time on related work.

The shape of the talk

The content has to fit the time, and your contribution is what the audience came for.

  • Focus on the problem, the challenges, the solution and the results.
  • Long context is rarely relevant and loses the audience halfway through.
  • Do not spend time justifying the research. Just state the problem being solved, clearly.
  • Try breaking the mold: instead of opening with an outline slide, open with strong motivation or with the problem. The talk becomes far more engaging.
  • Prepare backup slides after the closing slide, answering the questions you expect. You never show them during the talk, but they rescue the discussion.

How many slides? One slide per minute is a useful rule of thumb, more accurate for short talks than for long ones. A text-heavy slide eats more time and drains the audience.
